Incident Campaign Playbook and Chronicle

Overview

This page defines an "incident campaign": a coordinated, time‑bounded program to resolve recurring or prolonged service degradations that are not fixed by a single incident response. An incident campaign includes: detection and evidence collection, runbook execution, scheduled game‑days, on‑call rotation adjustments, customer status cadence, a sequence of mini‑postmortems, and an end‑of‑campaign synthesis. The playbook here is oriented around SLOs, signal-driven decisions, and minimizing customer impact while accelerating root cause elimination.

When to start a campaign

Start an incident campaign when one or more of the following are true:
- An SLO (error budget or P99 latency) has been repeatedly violated across multiple days despite normal incident handling.
- A service experiences >3 degradation incidents in a rolling 14‑day window with differing root causes.
- A systemic regression is suspected that spans infrastructure, serving runtime, or model quality and requires cross‑team remediation over multiple sprints.

Goals

Primary goals:
1) Restore SLO compliance and reduce customer visible incidents by 75% within the campaign window.
2) Eliminate at least one systemic root cause and deliver a permanent fix or compensating control.
3) Improve detection and runbook coverage to reduce time‑to‑ack by 50% for the next similar event.

Scope and timeline

- Typical campaign length: 2–6 weeks.
- Campaign phases: Kickoff (day 0), Active Remediation (weeks 0–3), Reinforcement & Game‑Days (weeks 2–5), Synthesis & Close (final 3 days).
- Scope is explicitly stated at kickoff and can be narrowed (but not broadened) without executive approval.

Campaign roles and ownership

- Campaign Lead (1): overall program manager; owns deadline, cadence, and communications (example: Leah Thompson).
- Technical Lead(s) (1–3): primary owners for engineering workstreams (examples: runtime, infra, model‑ops).
- SRE Lead (1): owns oncall adjustments, monitoring changes, and incident evidence collection (example: Marcus Kim).
- Customer Liaison (1): coordinates customer notices, answers SLAs questions, and owns the status page drafts (example: Diego Alvarez).
- Review Board (ad hoc): product, security, and release engineering stakeholders for high‑risk rollouts.

Kickoff checklist (Day 0)

1. Convene kickoff meeting (30–60 minutes) with all role owners. Agenda: definition of scope, target SLOs, campaign timeline, immediate mitigations.
2. Declare campaign on the internal channel: #incidents-campaigns with summary, scope, leads, and link to campaign doc.
3. Create a dedicated incident campaign page and channel; pin the campaign runbook.
4. Set short‑lived monitoring dashboards and alerts (see Monitoring adjustments).
5. Prepare customer status templates for phased updates (see Customer comms).

Signal & detection adjustments

During a campaign the SRE Lead should create temporary monitoring artifacts:
- Dedicated Grafana dashboard named "Campaign: <service> - <month>".
- Alerting windows tightened for critical signals (e.g., 1‑minute error rate alert increased to 3x baseline to catch bursts).
- Synthetic checks added at global egress points every 30s.
- Trace sampling rate increased for affected services to 100% for first 48 hours, then 20% thereafter.

Example Alert update (text snippet):
- Existing alert: "service-p99-latency-high" threshold 95th percentile > 800ms for 5m.
- Campaign update: set a temporary alert "campaign-service-p99-latency" threshold 800ms for 2m with runbook link.

Runbook execution and evidence collection

All oncall responders must:
1) Record precise timestamps for symptom onset and mitigation attempts.
2) Tag all traces and logs with campaign ID: CAMPAIGN-<YYYYMM>-<SERVICE>.
3) Create a running incident timeline in the campaign page with: symptom, who acted, commands run, and outcome.
4) Use the Evidence Checklist (below) and confirm artifacts are stored in the campaign evidence directory.

Evidence Checklist (plain list)
- Alert history (Grafana/PagerDuty) with timestamps.
- Traces (link to Jaeger/Tempo): include trace IDs.
- Logs (timebounded slices, stored in S3 path).
- Core dumps or heap dumps if applicable (with redaction).
- Deployment manifests and recent commits for the affected services.
- Load/capacity metrics (CPU, GPU, network).
- Customer error reports (support tickets) mapped to time windows.

Containment play patterns

Use containment actions to limit customer impact while preserving evidence:
- Traffic shaping: enable rate limiting or per‑tenant throttles.
- Reroute: failover to a fallback model or region if available.
- Autoscaling: provision burst instances with short TTLs.
- Feature toggle: revert or disable risky feature flags.

Example CLI snippets (for runbook use)
- Increase deployment replicas: kubectl scale deployment inference-svc --replicas=10 -n production
- Check rolling update status: kubectl rollout status deployment/inference-svc -n production
- Dump last 200 error logs: curl "http://logs.internal/api/query?service=inference-svc&limit=200&from=<start>&to=<end>"
- Tag a trace in Tempo: POST /api/tag {"trace_id": "<id>", "tag": "CAMPAIGN-202602-inference"}

Escalation and decision ladder

1. Oncall responder takes first containment steps and notifies SRE Lead.
2. SRE Lead assesses whether to escalate to Technical Lead(s).
3. If redeploys or rollbacks are required across regions, the Campaign Lead must be notified and approve cross‑team coordination.
4. For customer‑facing degradation > 6 hours or data integrity risks, raise to Review Board and initiate executive brief.

Customer comms cadence and templates

Customer Liaison is responsible for timed communications. Use the following staged templates and mark them on the status page. Timing is guideline—adjust based on impact.

Phase A: Acknowledgement (within 30 minutes of detection)
- Short status: "We are investigating an issue affecting [service area]. Our engineers are investigating. Impact: [who/where]."
- Internal root cause hint: omitted.

Phase B: Initial update (2–4 hours)
- Update: summary of observations, mitigations attempted, ETA for next update.
- Example: "We have identified increased 95th percentile inference latency for customers in eu‑west. We have scaled serving pools and are continuing to monitor. Next update in 2 hours."

Phase C: Ongoing progress (every 6–12 hours for prolonged events)
- Provide progress against campaign goals, scope of affected accounts, and mitigations planned.

Phase D: Post‑mitigation notice (when SLOs restored)
- Confirm restoration, summarize the root cause, and list next steps and timelines for permanent fixes.

Status page example entries (text)
- Title: Degraded inference latency (eu‑west)
- Impact: Some customers experienced elevated 95th percentile latencies.
- Root Cause: Memory pressure in runtime shard causing GC stalls (under investigation).
- Current Status: Under active remediation.
- Updates: 2026‑02‑04 04:12 UTC — Scaled serving pools; latency decreased by 30%.

Game‑day & reinforcement schedule

Game‑days are scheduled tests during the campaign to validate fixes and prevent regressions. Game‑day cadence: 2 scheduled runs spaced 4–7 days apart. Each game‑day includes:
- A focused failure injection scenario (e.g., KV cache eviction, high tail latency).
- A playbook dry run for oncall teams and incident commanders.
- A tabletop retro with actions captured and owners assigned.

Example game‑day script (simplified)
1. Simulate increased request rate from a synthetic client (scripted load generator).
2. Introduce a 30s delay on DB queries for one region only.
3. Observe alerting behavior and measure mean time to acknowledge (target <5 minutes).
4. Perform a controlled rollback of last deployment to validate fallback (if rollback is an approved mitigation).

Rotation and staffing adjustments

To preserve responder capacity during an active campaign:
- Appoint a Campaign Oncall (separate from normal oncall) to handle campaign coordination for 12 hour shifts.
- Reduce non‑critical oncall responsibilities (e.g., non‑campaign game‑days) for the SRE team.
- Provide dedicated shadowing slots to accelerate learning for new responders.

Postmortems and learning artifacts

Campaigns use a sequence of mini‑postmortems for each significant event or change and a final synthesis postmortem at close.

Mini‑postmortem template (complete within 72 hours):
- Title: Mini‑PM: <short description>
- Summary: one paragraph
- Timeline: bullet timestamps
- Contributing factors: bullets
- Immediate remediation: bullets
- Owner(s) and due date(s): bullets

Final synthesis postmortem (within 7 days of close):
- Executive summary and customer impact (quantified: affected accounts, outage minutes, credits).
- Root causes and system‑level contributing factors.
- Actions completed during campaign and outstanding actions with owners and dates.
- Runbook and monitoring changes adopted.
- Acceptance criteria confirming the campaign is closed (examples below).

Acceptance criteria for campaign close

1. Target SLOs observed for 7 consecutive days or demonstrable trend showing recovery to target within three business days post‑campaign.
2. All critical actions have owners and dates; at least one permanent fix merged or a compensating control in place.
3. Monitoring and alerting changes are reviewed and either made permanent or rolled back.
4. Customer follow‑up composed and sent to impacted accounts; account leads informed.

Retention and evidence handling

- All campaign evidence must be retained for 180 days in the campaign S3 bucket with access only to participants and Legal as needed.
- Sensitive artifacts (PII, HSM keys, secrets) must be redacted or referenced via secure audit logs only.

Example campaign timeline (text bullets)
- Day 0: Kickoff, monitoring, first customer ack sent.
- Day 1–3: Intensive investigation, 100% trace sampling, first game‑day run.
- Day 4–14: Targeted code fixes, capacity changes, two scheduled game‑days.
- Final 3 days: Synthesis, customer post, close.
